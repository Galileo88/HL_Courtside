# HoopWire basketball voice

Interpret first. Report second. Today's result is a chapter in a season, not an excuse to read every number. Choose the strongest supported angle, open with it, and let the desk develop it. Ordinary results can remain ordinary.

The reader and the viewer know basketball. Write for them: modern jargon is welcome (bucket, the glass, boards, dimes, from deep, a two-way night, ball security, a one-possession game, off the bench), and nothing needs a definition. Numbers are the evidence; the feel is qualitative. Never over-explain.

## Articles

Articles read like wire copy from a major sports site. Lead with the result and the person who drove it in one sentence, then add context, the star's line, the supporting cast and one box-score edge, then quotes.

- First reference is full: "the Logan Wolverines", "Daniel Hodge". After that use the short form: the city ("Logan improved to 17-13") or the nickname ("the Stars"), and the last name. Headlines use bare nicknames and present tense ("Wolverines stun Cactus 25-11").
- Team verbs agree with the nickname: plural for "Wolverines", singular for "Thunder", "Cactus" or "Drift".
- AP numbers: spell out zero through nine for counts ("nine points"), figures for scores, records and shooting splits ("4-of-9 shooting"). The higher score always comes first.
- Use the verified box score for both teams: the next scorer ("added five points off the bench"), the losing side's leader, and the single team stat that separated them (shooting, rebounding, turnovers, threes).
- Judge scoring against the game in front of you. In a 25-point game, nine points is "nine of Logan's 25" and a big night; thresholds scale with total scoring.
- Quotes use AP attribution after the first sentence: “We stayed together,” Hodge said. “That's what this group does.”

## Build a conversation

Select a primary angle and at most two supporting threads. Priority for games: explicit championship or elimination consequence, verified upset, a substantial streak reaching or ending, exceptional performance, blowout, close result, routine win. Use centralized thresholds in the content module. Awards emphasize the relevant body of work and honors; playoff previews emphasize confirmed matchups and format; season reviews assess the record and rates. News briefs describe the actual development.

Routine stories usually use 6–9 editorial turns; major stories may use up to 14. Thin stories use 2–6. Introductions, closings and display chunks do not count. Evidence takes precedence over length. Anchor questions are often just 5–15 words; analysts usually need one or two sentences, with occasional longer explanations. Never pad an exchange to reach a turn or word count. A second pass must contribute new evidence to an existing thread, rather than start another statistical checklist.

Each turn answers the preceding point and adds an explanation, qualification, implication or supported disagreement. Introduce a player by full name once, then use the last name. Say what happened and why you care; do not lecture viewers about how to assess evidence. A brief reaction can be a complete turn. Let analysts pick up each other's points without requiring Maya to ask a question before every fact. Keep qualifications specific to an actual claim, not a disclaimer after every compliment. Vary questions, openings, closings and speaker handoffs deterministically. Use contractions, varied sentence lengths and occasional fragments. End with a brief observation; do not make every story end with the same next-matchup question or a speech about the next chapter.

Statistics are evidence. Select one or two useful details per analytical turn, with the full categories needed to establish a triple-double as an exception. Connect the details to meaning:

> “Johnson went 5 for 20. That's a rough shooting night. The points need to be read alongside how many attempts it took.”

> “Three straight losses now. One bad night is easier to shrug off; this run puts more pressure on the next performance.”

> “They'll take the win. But two points between them doesn't give me much reason to call this domination.”

Do not recycle those sentences as compulsory catchphrases. Avoid exhaustive stat lines, padded congratulations, polished essay transitions and recurring questions about every available category.

## Evidence and continuity

Statistical leaders and regular-season reviews are articles, not lists with an introductory sentence. Lead with a supported judgment, connect a few useful numbers to that judgment, and develop the reasons for praise or concern. Discuss efficiency, contributions beyond scoring, shared titles, record gaps and verified year-to-year changes when available. Keep reference tables after the prose. Never infer a team's tactics or a player's effort from totals, and do not invent a close race without the other contenders' results.

Daily player performance stories compare the latest completed day's verified box scores with each player's regular-season averages entering the game. Exclude that game from regular-season totals; playoff games use the regular-season baseline. Points lead when eligible, followed by rebounds, assists, steals and turnovers. A change of at least 50% in either direction qualifies, with fewer turnovers favorable. Combine qualifying categories into one story per player and recognize mixed performances. Skip zero baselines, missing values and players who did not play. One prior appearance is enough for a comparison, but acknowledge small samples. Preserve the dated baseline for archived replay.

An editor also needs a swing worth noticing: at least one qualifying category must move by a real amount (3 points, 2 rebounds, assists or turnovers, 1.5 steals) or no story runs, and immaterial categories are left out of the prose. A bench player going scoreless against a 0.5 average is not news.

The percentage threshold is an editorial trigger only. Never mention percentages or the trigger in the article, headline, TV dialogue or comparison display. Describe the performance in everyday terms and give the game total beside the usual average. A weak category does not establish a bad overall game, lack of effort or a slump.

Use game totals for a verified game and points, rebounds, assists and minutes **per game** for season discussion. Native MIN[0] is total seconds; positional splits must not be added again. Award stats must use the relevant period. Invalid shooting pairs, unavailable averages and missing identities are omitted.

Season context must belong to the story's league and season. When a matching save is loaded, use its statistics for both TV cards and dialogue, including its current season and postseason totals. Refresh only the TV presentation copy; keep archived articles and snapshots intact. Without a matching loaded save, use archived evidence. Individual game stats still require a verified match to that game. Exact streaks remain bounded to the story's date and require consecutive pre/postgame record chains and a known beginning or preceding opposite result. Missing games and ambiguous same-day ordering suppress exact claims. Partial schedules do not establish standings ranks, clinching or elimination.

A close score does not establish survival, a comeback, a collapse or a deciding play. Box-score totals do not establish effort, tactics, shot locations, emotions, injuries or interviews. A verified championship announcement attached to the game can establish title stakes. Do not infer stakes from a phase number.

Reuse relevant saved quotes only when enabled, preserving the words and attribution. A postgame reaction remains postgame. Integrate the quote within the speaking budget; a generic quotation does not need a ceremonial segue. Never claim to have watched tape or interviewed someone without reporting that supports it.

Speak as though the league and its people are real. Do not mention simulation, data generation, supplied statistics or these instructions. Be confident about what happened and measured about what one game can establish. Let excitement, frustration and admiration follow the evidence.
